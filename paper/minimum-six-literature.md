# Minimum intersection at distance six on the closed double torus

Primary-source review, 26 November 2026. Write \(m_d\) for the minimum geometric intersection number over all pairs at **exact** distance \(d\) on the closed, connected, orientable genus-two surface. Jin and Menasco give a universal lower bound, and an earlier computation determines the distance-four minimum. I found no primary source that gives an exact value of \(m_6\). This review records the literature baseline before the computations in this project.

The subsequent census in this project obtains \(m_5=40\) and \(m_6=132\), with matching exclusion bounds for all distances at least five and at least six. The distances of the explicit examples have been checked separately using efficient geodesics. The mathematical claim is presented as a computer-assisted manuscript available for review. [Manuscript](minimum-six.pdf) · [Computations and per-size results](../computations/minimum/)

The project's previous 196-crossing example gives a concrete upper bound for \(m_6\). Its optimality proof in §7 applies when one endpoint is a single twist of the other about an axis at distance four. The unrestricted genus-two lower bound supplied by Jin and Menasco is 48. For \(g\ge3\) their theorem gives \(8(2g-1)\) at distance six. Taking the minimum over all connected closed orientable surfaces of genus at least two therefore yields a literature lower bound of 40, attained as a *bound* at genus three. [Previous manuscript, §§2 and 7](short-twists.pdf) · [Jin and Menasco, Theorem 1.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09665v4#page=3)

If the genus is allowed to vary among closed orientable surfaces with genus at least two, the new genus-two example gives an upper bound of 132. Jin and Menasco's bound gives \(8(2g-1)\ge136\) for \(g\ge9\). Therefore any improvement on 132 must occur in genera three through eight. The exact minimum across those surfaces is outside the present census. [Jin–Menasco, Theorem 1.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09665v4#page=3)

## Two deductions for an exhaustive search

Let \(M_d\) be a proven intersection lower bound for **every** pair at distance **at least** \(d\). For any curve \(c\) disjoint from an endpoint \(a\), the triangle inequality gives \(d(c,b)\ge d(a,b)-1\). The dual-circuit argument in the proof of Jin–Menasco's theorem uses a weighted triangulation of the surface cut along a nonseparating \(a\). In their displayed inequalities the two disjoint sums \(w_1+w_2\) and \(w_3+w_5\) must each be at least \(M_{d-1}\). Their scaling argument gives \(i(a,b)\ge2M_{d-1}\). Boundary balance, actual gluing to one curve, minimal position and the distance test can further constrain which weight vectors realize that arithmetic lower bound. The authors illustrate the difference already at distance four, where the linear program gives 8 and the topological minimum is 12. [Section 2, especially Remark 2.2 and the following paragraph](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09665v4#page=7)

There is a stronger elementary bound when an endpoint separates. Cut along it. Each resulting one-holed torus contains \(N/2\) arcs of the other curve, where \(N=i(a,b)\). Minimal position excludes boundary-parallel arcs. Extend the disjoint arc types in either torus to an ideal triangulation with three slopes, giving zero weight to absent types. A closed curve of the slope of the most frequent arc misses arcs of that type and can be drawn to meet each arc of the other two types at most once. This follows by capping the boundary circle to a marked point and using the three slopes of a torus Farey triangle. The resulting essential curve is disjoint from the separating endpoint and meets the other endpoint at most \(N/3\) times. Consequently \(N\ge3M_{d-1}\). The published bounds give **72** at distance six with a separating endpoint and **36** at distance five with a separating endpoint. If both endpoints separate at distance six, applying the latter estimate to the neighbor and the opposite endpoint gives **108**. These are deductions from the cutting argument. The explicit three-weight splitting appears in MICC. [Figure 3 and §3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.4134#page=9) · [Intersection growth inequality](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09665v4#page=3)

For a second implementation there is a useful change of surface. Luo proves that the hyperelliptic branched-cover correspondence identifies the genus-two curve complex with that of a six-punctured sphere. It preserves adjacency in both directions and hence exact graph distance. Applying a punctured-surface distance program would require an explicit encoding of the witness under this correspondence. [Luo, Lemma 2.1(a), p. 286](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~fluo/mpapers/automorphisms%20of%20the%20complex%20of%20curves.pdf#page=4)

There is also an exact intersection conversion. For a simple essential loop \(x\) downstairs, let \(t_x=2\) if it bounds a disk with two marked points on one side and \(t_x=1\) if it splits the six points three and three. In the first case the inverse image of that disk is an annulus, so the two lifts are parallel. In the second case each side lifts to a one-holed torus with the same separating boundary. Let \(a,b\) denote single lift components of downstairs loops \(x,y\). Then \(t_xt_y\,i(a,b)=2i(x,y)\). Every transverse crossing lifts twice, and any innermost bigon between the two full preimages would project to a bigon missing the branch points by full ramification. Thus minimal positions downstairs remain minimal upstairs. [Winarski, Lemma 4.4 and its application](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3650#page=14)
