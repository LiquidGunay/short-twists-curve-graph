# Where distance six was known, and what the short twists add

Research note, 25 November 2026. Throughout, the surface is closed, connected and orientable, of genus at least two. A *seed* is an ordered pair of curves $a,b$ at curve-graph distance four. Write $T_b$ for a Dehn twist about $b$, $D_k=d(a,T_b^k a)$, and $i(a,b)$ for the least number of crossings of representatives of those two curves.

## Where to look for the original results

| Claim | Earliest source or direct construction located |
|---|---|
| Vertices at every exact distance exist | The infinite-diameter argument is traced to **Tsuyoshi Kobayashi, 1988, Proposition 2.2**. Masur and Schleimer identify that proposition in the proof of their Lemma 2.5. Hempel explicitly credits Feng Luo with pointing out the argument implicit in Kobayashi. Masur and Minsky state infinite diameter in Theorem 1.1 of their October 1996 manuscript, published in 1999. On a connected graph of infinite diameter, choose a sufficiently long shortest path and stop after the sixth edge, or the $n$-th. This last step is a deduction. ## What Mahanta's argument contributed

The operation fixes the twist axis $b$ and compares the original $a$ to its image after one turn. Mahanta and Palaparthi proved that the answer is always four when the input curves are three edges apart. At input distance four, Mahanta bounded the answer between four and six. Her Lemma 9 detects a four-edge path through a curve satisfying a condition on the arrangement of strands in the twisting collar, called the stacking property. Her Theorem 2 narrows the test to a particular kind of curve when the complement of the input has no hexagons. [Joint paper, Theorem 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.topol.2022.108137) · [Solo paper, Lemma 9 and Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15290v3#page=19)

The genus-two example in Figure 18 does have hexagons. For that diagram Mahanta exhibited a five-edge route and ruled out four edges by a separate analysis. This gave explicit endpoints at distance five with just 144 intersections. Our finite search answers the next question about the same diagram by ruling out five edges for its double and triple twists. For the single twist it also locates the first starting intersection count that can attain six on this surface. [Mahanta's example, §6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15290v3#page=19) · [Our Theorems 1 and 4](short-twists.pdf)

## The two directions of the conjecture

Let $M$ mean that the seed attains the *minimum among all distance-four pairs in its genus*, and $F$ mean that one twist gives distance five. The thesis makes this reading explicit by writing $i(a,b)=i_{\min}(g,4)$. Every diagram here already realizes the geometric intersection number of its own isotopy classes. The word “only” changes which implication is being conjectured. [Thesis, Question 4](https://gyan.iitg.ac.in/server/api/core/bitstreams/5ff9c20a-8ec9-40de-b253-4c1d153e1534/content#page=19)

| Text | Exact logical direction | Outcome on the closed genus-two surface |
|---|---|---|
| Mahanta v2, 30 March 2023, p. 2 | $M\Rightarrow F$ | Holds. Every 12-crossing distance-four seed in the census has $D_1=5$. [Preprint v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15290v2#page=2) |
| Mahanta v3, 3 August 2023, p. 2, immediately before Question 1 | $F\Rightarrow M$ | False. A 13-crossing seed has $D_1=5$ although the minimum is 12. [Attached preprint v3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15290v3#page=2) |
| Mahanta's IIT Guwahati thesis, §1.2, Question 4, printed p. 6 | Again asks whether $M\Rightarrow F$ | The title page says 2023, and the department records the viva on 12 January 2024. The same page also suggests testing the expression $d(a,T_{T_c a}a)$ when $d(a,c)=3$. For the four-square example in the revised manuscript we obtain five. [Thesis p. 6](https://gyan.iitg.ac.in/server/api/core/bitstreams/5ff9c20a-8ec9-40de-b253-4c1d153e1534/content#page=19) · [IIT Guwahati viva record](https://www.iitg.ac.in/maths/index_thesis_details.php) |

Question 1 of v3 is broader than either implication. It asks which positive powers give distance six **for a given arbitrary distance-four pair**. For Figure 18 the exact answer is every $k\ge2$. One twist gives five by Mahanta. Our finite calculations give six for $k=2,3$. For all $k\ge4$, §3 of the manuscript gives a uniform bound from Masur and Minsky's annular projection formula. This settles the named example and leaves the general small-power classification open in this work. [Mahanta v3, Question 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15290v3#page=2) · [Masur–Minsky II, §2.4, equation (2.6)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9807150#page=17)

## The contribution supported by the computations

The long-term aim in Mahanta's introduction was to build distance $n+1$ from distance $n$. If this means *apply one twist to every seed*, the 2022 theorem works at $n=3$, and the 14-crossing example shows the proposed rule fails at $n=4$, where one twist can jump from four to six. If it means *construct at least one pair at each distance*, the earlier geodesic constructions already accomplish it. [Mahanta v2, introduction](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15290v2#page=2) · [Mahanta–Palaparthi, Theorem 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.topol.2022.108137)

The complete genus-two enumeration in §7 of the revised manuscript gives five ordered distance-four classes at 12 crossings and eleven at 13, allowing homeomorphisms of either orientation. Every one has $D_1=5$. At 14 crossings we exhibit a seed with $D_1=6$. Therefore **14 is the exact minimum starting intersection for a distance-four seed whose single twist gives six**. The endpoints then intersect $14^2=196$ times. Higher powers give at least $2\cdot12^2=288$ crossings. The twist example and the published intersection growth inequality gave the interval $48\le i_{\min}(2,6)\le196$. The subsequent unrestricted census determines $i_{\min}(2,6)=132$. [MICC, distance-four minimum](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.4134#page=5) · [Jin–Menasco, Theorem 1.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09665v4#page=3) · [Minimum-intersection paper](minimum-six.pdf)

The historical obstacle is the lower bound on distance. A six-edge walk is easy to draw. A single overlooked five-edge walk disproves the proposed equality, and each vertex has infinitely many neighbors. A large twist supplies a uniform obstruction by subsurface projection. At powers two and three its elementary annular bound still permits a shortcut. The finite search uses the efficient-geodesic theorem to certify that it has checked enough possible first moves. [Birman–Margalit–Menasco, Theorem 1.1 and §3.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.4133)
